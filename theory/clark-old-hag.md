## Verbatim (short, attributed)
- "The 'Old Hag' is a folk expression — popular, for example, in Newfoundland — for the particular experience that gave rise to the word 'nightmare.'"
- Etymology: "nightmare (from the Anglo-Saxon nicht [night] and mara [incubus or succubus]) referred to a specific nocturnal experience."
- Shape: "A menacing supernatural entity, often perceived as an ugly witch, enters a bedroom and sits on the witness's chest, leaving him or her with the sensation of being crushed. All the while the victim lies paralyzed and helpless."
- Frequency cited: "one in six, according to a scientist who has studied the phenomenon."
- Hufford's charge: explainers produced "an effort to reinvent the experience so that it could be 'explained.'" And: "one can hardly distinguish the experiences themselves from their interpretations" (via Jones).

## Paraphrased points
- US culture has no name for it → sufferers doubt sanity in isolation.
- Hufford (Penn State folklorist + medical scientist), The Terror That Comes in the Night (1982): uses the case to scrutinize debunking habits (perceptual error, faulty memory, lies, psychosis, culture-shaped hallucination) and to defend witness-testimony trustworthiness.
- Early psychoanalyst Ernest Jones's study is the foil Hufford answers.
- Visual anchor: Fuseli, The Nightmare, 1781 (Detroit Institute).
